First, the right request

A repair and a replacement are different requests. The form reads them differently — in both directions.

The form is short, but what you pick in it decides how the request is read. Send a furnace in for replacement and it gets priced as a new furnace — the largest bill a homeowner pays — when the fault may only be a part. Send a replacement in as a repair and it gets priced like a repair — a part and a visit — and you are back where you started. Pick the job the furnace actually is, and the price that comes back is the price you were trying to compare.

  1. Job: pick the one that is actually true

    In the “What do you need?” menu, “Furnace repair” is for a furnace that still heats the house and is misbehaving — short-cycling, an error code, one cold room. “Furnace replacement” is for one you have decided to change out. “No heat — urgent” is for one that has stopped heating the house altogether — and if the house is cold right now, that is the request to send, not this page. The two single-intent pages are furnace repair and furnace replacement.

  2. Timing: say what is actually true

    The “How soon?” menu tells the contractor whether the furnace is still keeping the house warm while you wait, or the house is cold and this is a today job. If the house is cold, that is a no-heat request and this site routes those first.

  3. Description: what it is doing, and how old it is

    The description box is what a contractor reads before deciding whether to call. Say what the furnace actually does, how much of the house is warm, and the make and rough age if you know them. If a technician has already looked at it and told you what it needs, put that in your own words.

For example: the two requests read differently on purpose. “Furnace still heats, but it short-cycles every few minutes and the panel shows an error code. Two-storey house, the upstairs never gets warm — looking for a repair quote before I decide anything.” Or, on the other side: “Furnace is a mid-1990s unit and still heats, but the repair quote came back close to the price of a new one — looking for a replacement quote.” That kind of detail is what gets a real number instead of a call back to ask questions you could have answered in the box.

What to ask for

What each written quote should contain.

We do not do the work and we do not quote for it, so there is no price on this site. A licensed contractor quotes you. What you can do is hold a repair quote and a replacement quote to the same list, so the two numbers mean the same thing before you compare them.

A written repair quote should contain

  • The price, in writing, before work starts. Ask for it by email so there is a record of what was quoted, not just what was said on the phone.
  • Whether the diagnostic or trip charge is separate. Many contractors charge to come out and diagnose before any repair is agreed. Ask what that charge is, and whether it comes off the repair price if you go ahead.
  • What the part is, and why it is the one being replaced. Ask for the part named in writing, so a second opinion can tell whether the same fault was found twice.
  • What is covered after the repair, and for how long. Ask whether the part and the labour carry any guarantee, and for how long. Know it before you agree, not after.
  • The contractor's Alberta licence. Ask for the licence number and check the contractor's standing before you sign. A licensed contractor hands it over without blinking.

A written replacement quote should contain

  • The price, in writing, before work starts. Ask for it by email so there is a record of what was quoted, not just what was said on the phone.
  • Whether the permit is included. Ask whether the price covers the permit for the installation, and who pulls it.
  • Whether removal and disposal of the old furnace is included. It is a separate line on some quotes and built into others — either way, know which one you are looking at.
  • What make, model and size is being quoted. Two prices only compare if you know what equipment each one is for.
  • The contractor's Alberta licence. Ask for the licence number and check the contractor's standing before you sign. A licensed contractor hands it over without blinking.

What happens after you send it

It reaches contractors who work in your postal code. They call you with a price.

  1. You send the request

    Name, phone, postal code and what the furnace is doing. That is the whole form. It has no server — it builds your request and opens it in your own email app, and nothing leaves your browser until you press Send there.

  2. It goes to heating contractors who work in your part of Edmonton

    The postal code you give decides which side of the city that is, and the job you picked decides which contractors it suits. Not a national call centre, and not a blast to fifteen companies.

  3. You get called with a price

    Expect a call or a text. Ask for the price in writing, and ask the questions above before you agree. You owe nobody anything for the quote.
